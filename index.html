Unit introduction

Unit 10: General Wave Properties

Think about a small stone dropped into a canal or pond. Rings spread out across the water. Think about the sound of the Azaan from a mosque reaching your home. Think about a swing (jhoola) moving to and fro in a village garden. All of these are connected to waves and vibrations.

Waves you already know

Water ripples

Rings spreading on a pond after a stone falls.

Sound

The Azaan, a cricket commentary on the radio, or a shout in a valley.

Rope waves

A rope shaken up and down makes a hump that runs along it.

Light and radio

Sunlight, TV signals and your mobile phone signal.

Why are waves important?

Waves carry energy and information from one place to another. Without waves we could not hear, see, watch TV or use a mobile phone. Earthquakes also travel as waves. So waves are one of the most important ideas in Physics.

Extra help: prerequisites

Get ready: Physics ideas you need

Let us first learn the small words that we will use again and again. Take your time. Every word here is simple.

Vibration (oscillation)

A to-and-fro motion. A swing, a guitar string and a ruler pressed on a desk and released all vibrate.

Mean position

The rest position in the middle, where the object stays when nothing disturbs it. Also called the equilibrium position.

Displacement

How far an object is from its mean position, in a stated direction. Its SI unit is the metre (m).

Medium

The material through which a wave travels, such as a rope, water or air.

Particle

A tiny piece of the medium. Water is made of very many tiny particles.

Energy

The ability to do work. Its SI unit is the joule (J). A wave carries energy.

Extra help: Maths

Get ready: your Maths toolkit

Wave problems need only a few simple Maths skills. Let us practise them now.

1. Changing units

Our formulae need SI units. So change centimetres to metres first.

Rule

cm to m: divide by 100. Example: 50 cm = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5 m.
mm to m: divide by 1000. Example: 20 mm = 0.02 m.

2. Rearranging a formula (changing the subject)

Look at v = f × λ. If we multiply two numbers to get v, then dividing v by one of them gives back the other.

  1. Start: v = f × λ
  2. To get λ alone, divide both sides by f: v ÷ f = (f × λ) ÷ f
  3. The f on the right cancels: v ÷ f = λ. So λ = v ÷ f.

Simple rule: what is multiplied on one side goes to the other side as division, and the opposite.

3. Reciprocal (flip a number)

The reciprocal of a number is 1 divided by that number. For example, 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25, and 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2. We use this for T = 1/f.

4. Squares and square roots

3² = 3 × 3 = 9. The square root undoes it: √9 = 3. Also √0.01 = 0.1 because 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01. We need square roots for pendulum and spring formulae. Use the √ key on your calculator.

5. The number π (pi)

π ≈ 3.14 (more exactly 3.1416). So 2π ≈ 6.28 and 4π² ≈ 39.48. Use the π key on your calculator for better accuracy.

6. Rounding

Round your final answer sensibly, usually to 2 or 3 significant figures. Keep extra digits during the middle steps.

Textbook topic: Waves

What is a wave?

Start with something you can see

Drop a stone into a pond. A cork floating on the water bobs up and down. The ripple moves outward across the pond. The cork does not travel to the bank with the ripple. So what moved forward? The disturbance moved, and it carried energy.

The wave moves to the right. The cork only moves up and down. Watch it for a few seconds.

Why does this happen?

Each water particle is connected to its neighbours. When one particle is pushed, it pulls and pushes the next one. That one moves the next one, and so on. Each particle only vibrates around its own place. But the motion is passed on, so the disturbance travels.

Daily-life picture

In a cricket stadium, people make a “Mexican wave”. Each person only stands up and sits down in his own seat. But the wave runs round the whole stadium. People do not move around, yet the wave does.

Definition: Wave

A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium (or through space) and transfers energy from one place to another, without the medium as a whole moving along with it.

Key idea

Every wave starts from a vibrating source (a hand shaking a rope, a vibrating tuning fork, a stone hitting water). A wave carries energy, not matter.

Textbook topic: Mechanical / Electromagnetic waves

Mechanical and electromagnetic waves

Waves are placed into two big families. The difference is simple: does the wave need a medium?

Mechanical waves

Mechanical waves need a material medium (solid, liquid or gas) to travel. The particles of the medium pass on the vibration.

Examples: sound waves, water waves, waves on a rope or spring, earthquake (seismic) waves.

Electromagnetic waves

Electromagnetic (EM) waves do not need a medium. They can travel through empty space (a vacuum). In a vacuum they all travel at about 3 × 108 m/s.

Examples: light, radio waves, microwaves, X-rays, the signal of a mobile phone.

Mechanical wavesElectromagnetic waves
Medium needed?YesNo
Can travel in a vacuum?NoYes
ExamplesSound, water ripples, rope wavesLight, radio waves, X-rays
Type of waveTransverse or longitudinalTransverse
Why can we see the Sun but not hear it?

Between the Sun and the Earth there is mostly empty space. Light (an EM wave) can cross empty space, so sunlight reaches us. Sound (a mechanical wave) needs a medium, so it cannot cross empty space.

Textbook topic: Transverse waves (rope waves, water waves)

Transverse waves

The rope experiment

Tie one end of a long rope to a pole. Hold the other end and move your hand up and down once. A hump runs along the rope towards the pole. Look at a small piece of the rope. It only moves up and down. The hump moves forward.

A transverse wave. The wave travels to the right. The rope particles move up and down, at right angles to the wave direction.
Definition: Transverse wave

A wave in which the particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction in which the wave travels.

Watch the particles

The red particle only moves up and down. The shape of the wave moves to the right.

Crest and trough

  • Crest: the highest point of the wave, above the rest line.
  • Trough: the lowest point of the wave, below the rest line.

Examples of transverse waves: waves on a rope, ripples on the surface of water (textbook treats them as transverse), and all electromagnetic waves such as light.

Common mix-up

Do not think the rope moves forward with the wave. The rope stays where it is. Only the shape (the hump) travels.

Textbook topic: Longitudinal (compressional) waves, sound

Longitudinal waves

The spring experiment

Take a long spring (a slinky) lying on a table. Hold one end and push it forward and pull it back along its length. You will see regions where the coils are squeezed together and regions where they are stretched apart. These regions travel along the spring.

A longitudinal wave. Each line is one particle. Particles vibrate to and fro along the same line as the wave travels.
Definition: Longitudinal wave

A wave in which the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to (along) the direction in which the wave travels. It is also called a compressional wave.

TermMeaning
CompressionA region where the particles are close together (high pressure or density).
RarefactionA region where the particles are far apart (low pressure or density). It is the stretched region.

Watch the particles

Look at the red particle. It moves left and right, along the direction of the wave. Notice where the lines bunch up (compression) and spread out (rarefaction).
Sound is a longitudinal wave

When a loudspeaker cone moves forward it squeezes the air in front of it (compression). When it moves back, the air spreads out (rarefaction). These compressions and rarefactions travel through the air to your ear. So sound in air is a longitudinal wave.

Textbook topic: Characteristics of waves (wavelength, amplitude)

Amplitude and wavelength

To describe a wave, we measure it. Look at this diagram carefully. Every label is explained below.

Amplitude is measured from the rest line to a crest (or to a trough). Wavelength is measured from one crest to the next crest.
Definition: Amplitude (a)

The maximum displacement of a particle from its mean (rest) position. SI unit: metre (m).

Simple meaning: how high the crest rises above the rest line. A bigger amplitude means the wave carries more energy (a louder sound, a taller water wave).

Definition: Wavelength (λ)

The distance between two consecutive crests (or two consecutive troughs). In a longitudinal wave, it is the distance between two consecutive compressions (or two consecutive rarefactions). SI unit: metre (m).

Simple meaning: the length of one complete wave.

Careful

Amplitude is not the distance from trough to crest. That distance is twice the amplitude. And wavelength is not crest to trough. It is crest to the next crest.

Textbook topic: Period, frequency, displacement–time graph

Time period and frequency

Now let us ask: how fast does a particle vibrate? Watch one particle of a wave. It goes up, comes down, goes below, and comes back to where it started. That is one complete oscillation.

Definition: Time period (T)

The time taken for one complete oscillation (or for one complete wave to pass a point). SI unit: second (s).

Definition: Frequency (f)

The number of complete oscillations (or complete waves) in one second. SI unit: hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 oscillation per second.

اردو میں سمجھیں

فریکوئنسی کا مطلب ہے: ایک سیکنڈ میں کتنی مکمل لہریں گزرتی ہیں۔

Reading a displacement–time graph

This graph shows how one particle moves as time passes. It is our own example (your textbook also has a displacement–time graph, Fig. A).

The graph repeats itself every 0.4 s. So T = 0.4 s. The highest point is 10 cm, so the amplitude a = 10 cm.
  1. Amplitude: read the highest value on the vertical axis. It is 10 cm.
  2. Period: find the time for one full repeat, from one crest to the next crest. The first crest is at 0.1 s and the next at 0.5 s. So T = 0.5 − 0.1 = 0.4 s.
  3. Frequency: use f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5 Hz. So there are 2.5 oscillations every second.
Formula: link between T and f
T = 1f   and   f = 1T

T = time period (s). f = frequency (Hz). If one is big, the other is small. A fast vibration has a high frequency and a short period.

Rearranging: T = 1/f. Multiply both sides by f: T × f = 1. Divide both sides by T: f = 1/T.

Example from home

Electricity in Pakistan is alternating current with frequency 50 Hz. That means 50 complete cycles every second. Time for one cycle: T = 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s.

Textbook topic: Wave velocity (speed), frequency, wavelength

Wave speed: v = f λ

Where does the formula come from?

Speed = distance ÷ time. A wave moves forward one wavelength (λ) in one time period (T). So:

v = λT

But 1/T is the frequency f. So we can write:

Wave equation
v = f × λ
SymbolQuantitySI unit
vwave speed (velocity)metre per second (m/s)
ffrequencyhertz (Hz)
λwavelengthmetre (m)

Unit check: Hz × m = (1/s) × m = m/s ✓.

When can we use it? For every kind of wave: sound, water, rope, light.

Step-by-step rearrangement

To findStarting from v = f λFormula
Speed valready alonev = f × λ
Frequency fdivide both sides by λf = v ÷ λ
Wavelength λdivide both sides by fλ = v ÷ f
Who decides what?

The medium decides the wave speed v. The source decides the frequency f. Then λ = v ÷ f follows. If you shake a rope faster (bigger f) on the same rope (same v), the wavelength gets smaller.

Example 1: finding the speed
Question
A wave on a rope has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength 0.4 m. Find its speed.
Given
f = 5 Hz, λ = 0.4 m
Required
v = ?
Formula
v = f × λ
Why this formula?
We know f and λ and want the speed. The wave equation connects exactly these three.
Substitution
v = 5 × 0.4
Calculation
5 × 0.4 = 2.0
Answer
v = 2 m/s
Check
5 waves pass each second, each 0.4 m long. So the wave moves 5 × 0.4 = 2 m in 1 s. ✓ Reasonable for a rope wave.
Example 2: finding the wavelength
Question
A sound wave travels in air at 340 m/s. Its frequency is 170 Hz. Find its wavelength.
Given
v = 340 m/s, f = 170 Hz
Required
λ = ?
Formula
λ = v ÷ f (from v = f λ, dividing both sides by f)
Why this formula?
λ is alone on the left, and v and f are known.
Substitution
λ = 340 ÷ 170
Calculation
170 × 2 = 340, so 340 ÷ 170 = 2
Answer
λ = 2 m
Check
Put back: f × λ = 170 × 2 = 340 m/s ✓. A wavelength of 2 m is normal for a low-pitched sound.

Question
Water ripples have wavelength 50 cm and frequency 20 Hz. Find their speed.

Example 4: a multi-step problem
Question
A float on a pond bobs up and down 12 times in 6 s. The distance between neighbouring crests is 1.5 m. Find (a) the frequency, (b) the time period, (c) the wave speed.
Given
12 oscillations in t = 6 s, λ = 1.5 m
Required
f, T and v
(a) Frequency
f = number of oscillations ÷ time = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 Hz
(b) Time period

(c) Speed

Another way: v = λ ÷ T = 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = 3 m/s ✓. Both methods agree.

Textbook topic: Ripple tank, plane and circular waves

The ripple tank and wavefronts

Water waves are slow and big. So we can study them easily. For this we use a ripple tank. Next four lessons (reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference) all use it.

A ripple tank. The motor vibrates the dipper. The lamp shines light through the water. The screen shows a bright and dark pattern of the waves.
  1. A shallow tray is filled with a little water. It is held on elastic bands so that outside shaking does not disturb it.
  2. A vibrator (motor) moves a bar, called a dipper, up and down in the water. It makes the waves.
  3. A lamp above shines light through the water onto a white screen below.
  4. The wave pattern on the screen shows the wavefronts as bright and dark lines.
Definition: Wavefront

A line (or surface) joining all the points of a wave that are in the same state of vibration, for example the line along a crest. The wave travels at right angles to its wavefronts. The distance between two neighbouring wavefronts is one wavelength.

Plane waves and circular waves

A straight (plane) dipper gives straight wavefronts. A small spherical (point) dipper gives circular wavefronts.

Textbook topic: Reflection (water waves at a straight barrier)

Reflection of waves

Throw a ball at a wall. It bounces back. Waves also bounce back when they hit a barrier. We see this in the ripple tank by placing a straight barrier in the water.

Definition: Reflection

The bouncing back of a wave when it strikes a barrier (a boundary). The wave stays in the same medium.

Incident waves hit the barrier. Reflected waves leave it. The normal is a line at 90° to the barrier.
TermMeaning
NormalThe dotted line drawn at 90° (right angle) to the barrier, at the point where the wave hits.
Angle of incidence (i)The angle between the incident wave direction and the normal.
Angle of reflection (r)The angle between the reflected wave direction and the normal.
Law of reflection
Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection   (i = r)

The incident wave, the reflected wave and the normal all lie in the same plane (the flat water surface).

What changes and what stays the same?

In reflection, the wave stays in the same water. So its speed, wavelength and frequency stay the same. Only the direction changes.

Everyday example: echo

Shout in front of a big wall or a mountain, far away. After a short time you hear your own voice again. The sound waves were reflected by the wall. This reflected sound is called an echo.

Common mistake

Angles are measured from the normal, not from the barrier. If a wave makes 30° with the barrier surface, the angle of incidence is 90° − 30° = 60°.

Textbook topic: Refraction (deep and shallow water)

Refraction of waves

Imagine you are riding a bicycle on a smooth road at an angle, and one wheel reaches soft sand. That wheel slows down first, and the bicycle turns. Waves behave in a similar way when their speed changes.

Definition: Refraction

The change in direction of a wave when it passes from one medium (or depth) into another, caused by the change in its speed.

In the ripple tank

A glass plate is placed in the tray. Water above the plate is shallow. Water elsewhere is deep. In shallow water there is more friction with the bottom. So the waves slow down.

Waves moving from deep water (top) into shallow water (bottom). The wavefronts become closer together, and the direction bends towards the normal.
What happens going from deep to shallow water
  • Speed decreases (more friction).
  • Wavelength decreases (wavefronts come closer).
  • Frequency stays the same.
  • If the wave hits the boundary at an angle, it bends towards the normal. If it hits head-on (along the normal) it does not bend.

Why does the wavelength decrease but the frequency stay the same?

The frequency is set by the source. The number of waves arriving at the boundary each second must equal the number leaving it each second. So f does not change. Now use v = f λ. If f stays the same and v becomes smaller, λ must become smaller by the same fraction.

Example: refraction numbers
Question
Water waves of frequency 4 Hz travel at 0.20 m/s in deep water and 0.12 m/s in shallow water. Find the wavelength in each.
Formula
λ = v ÷ f
Deep water
λ = 0.20 ÷ 4 = 0.05 m
Shallow water
λ = 0.12 ÷ 4 = 0.03 m
Check
The shallow wavelength is smaller ✓. Also 0.03 ÷ 0.05 = 0.6 and 0.12 ÷ 0.20 = 0.6, so λ decreased in the same ratio as v ✓.
Local example

At a beach like Clifton in Karachi, waves slow down and get closer together as they reach shallow water near the shore. Their direction also bends so that waves arrive nearly parallel to the shore.

Textbook topic: Diffraction (gaps, shadow regions)

Diffraction of waves

Have you noticed that you can hear a person talking around a corner, but you cannot see him? Sound waves bend around the corner. This bending and spreading is called diffraction.

Definition: Diffraction

The spreading (bending) of waves around the edges of an obstacle or when they pass through a gap.

Left: a wide gap, waves go mostly straight on and there are shadow regions at the sides. Right: a narrow gap, the waves spread out as circles.

What decides how much waves spread?

Compare the gap width with the wavelength.

Gap width compared with wavelengthWhat you see
Much wider than λVery little spreading. Waves go straight through. Shadow regions (calm water) on both sides.
About the same as λ, or smallerStrong diffraction. Circular waves spread out in all directions beyond the gap.

Wavelength and speed do not change in diffraction. Only the shape and direction of the wavefronts change.

Why can we hear around a corner but not see?

Sound waves in air have wavelengths of about a metre, which is similar to the width of a doorway. So they spread well. Light waves have extremely small wavelengths, far less than a thousandth of a millimetre. They hardly bend around everyday objects, so light seems to go in straight lines.

Textbook topic: Interference (two dippers in a ripple tank)

Interference of waves

What happens when two waves meet at the same place? Throw two stones into a pond near each other. The ripples cross each other and pass on, but where they cross, the water shape is a combination of both.

Principle of superposition

When two waves meet, the resulting displacement at each point is the sum of the displacements of the two waves at that point. (Upward displacements are positive, downward ones negative.)

Definition: Interference

The effect produced when two (or more) waves overlap in the same region and combine, giving a larger, smaller or zero displacement.

Left: crest meets crest and the waves add. Right: crest meets trough and the waves cancel.
TypeWhen it happensResult
Constructive interferenceCrest meets crest (or trough meets trough)Larger amplitude. Displacements add.
Destructive interferenceCrest meets troughSmaller amplitude. Zero if the two waves are equal.
Number example

A crest of height 3 cm meets another crest of 2 cm. Result: 3 + 2 = 5 cm (constructive). A crest of +3 cm meets a trough of −3 cm. Result: 3 + (−3) = 0 cm (destructive).

Two dippers in a ripple tank

Two small dippers on the same vibrator make two sets of circular waves with the same frequency. Where the waves overlap, a steady pattern forms.

S₁ and S₂ are the two dippers. Along the solid red lines the waves arrive crest-with-crest and the water moves strongly. Along the dashed blue lines crest meets trough and the water stays almost calm.
Energy is not destroyed

In destructive interference, energy does not disappear. It goes to the places where interference is constructive. The total energy stays the same.

Textbook topic: Simple harmonic motion

Simple harmonic motion (SHM)

Every wave begins with a vibrating source. Now we study the vibration itself. The simplest and most important vibration is called simple harmonic motion.

A mass on a spring

A block of mass m is joined to a spring of stiffness k and rests on a smooth table. At position O the spring is neither stretched nor squeezed. This is the mean position (x = 0). Pull the block to B and let go. It moves to O, goes on to A, comes back, and keeps repeating.

The block moves to and fro between A and B, passing through O. The displacement x is measured from O.

Why does it come back?

When the spring is stretched or squeezed it pulls or pushes the block back towards O. This force is called the restoring force. It always points towards the mean position.

Hooke’s law (restoring force of a spring)
F = − k x

F = restoring force (N). k = spring constant, the stiffness of the spring (N/m). x = displacement from the mean position (m). The minus sign means the force acts opposite to the displacement, that is, towards O.

A stiffer spring has a larger k. Since F = m a, the acceleration is a = −(k/m) x.

Definition: Simple harmonic motion

SHM is the to-and-fro motion of a body about its mean position, in which the acceleration (or restoring force) is directly proportional to the displacement and is always directed towards the mean position.

Examples of SHM: a mass on a spring, a simple pendulum swinging through a small angle, a ball rolling to and fro in a bowl, a vibrating tuning fork prong.

Mass–spring system: time period

How long does one oscillation take? Think about two things: a heavy block is hard to speed up, so it moves slowly. A stiff spring pulls strongly, so it makes the block move quickly.

Time period of a mass–spring system
T = 2π mk
SymbolQuantitySI unit
Ttime periodsecond (s)
mmass of the blockkilogram (kg)
kspring constantnewton per metre (N/m)

Also f = 1/T. Valid for an ideal spring with no friction. Please compare with the form given in your textbook.

  • Bigger mass m → longer period T (slower).
  • Stiffer spring (bigger k) → shorter period T (faster).
  • The amplitude does not change T. A big swing and a small swing take the same time for each oscillation.

Try it yourself

0.5 kg
50 N/m
T = 0.63 s
Try this

Make the mass 4 times bigger. What happens to T? (It becomes 2 times longer, because √4 = 2.)

Rearranging the formula

  1. Start: T = 2π√(m/k)
  2. Divide both sides by 2π: T/(2π) = √(m/k)
  3. Square both sides: T²/(4π²) = m/k
  4. To get k: k = 4π²m / T². To get m: m = kT² / 4π².
Example 1: finding T and f
Question
A 0.2 kg block is attached to a spring of spring constant 80 N/m. Find the time period and the frequency.
Given
m = 0.2 kg, k = 80 N/m
Required
T and f
Formula
T = 2π√(m/k), then f = 1/T
Why?
It is a mass on a spring, and we know m and k.
Substitution
T = 2π × √(0.2 ÷ 80)
Calculation
0.2 ÷ 80 = 0.0025. √0.0025 = 0.05. So T = 2 × 3.1416 × 0.05 = 0.31416 s. Then f = 1 ÷ 0.31416 = 3.183 Hz.

T ≈ 0.31 s and f ≈ 3.2 Hz
Check
3.2 oscillations per second means each takes about 0.31 s ✓.

Textbook topic: Simple pendulum (forces on the bob)

The simple pendulum

Definition: Simple pendulum

A small heavy ball (the bob) hung from a rigid support by a long, light, thin thread. The length L is the distance from the point of suspension to the centre of the bob.

Pull the bob a little to one side and release it. It swings to and fro about its equilibrium (mean) position, the straight-down position. This is a very good example of SHM when the angle is small (less than about 10°).

Forces on the bob when it is pulled aside by angle θ. (The angle is drawn large so you can see it.)

Forces on the bob

  • Weight mg acts straight down.
  • We split mg into two parts: mg cosθ along the thread (pulling the bob away from the support) and mg sinθ at right angles to the thread (along the swing path).
  • Tension T acts along the thread towards the support. At the extreme position the bob is momentarily at rest, so mg cosθ = T. These two balance.
  • The part mg sinθ is not balanced. It pulls the bob back towards the equilibrium position. It is the restoring force.
Time period of a simple pendulum
T = 2π Lg
SymbolQuantitySI unit
Ttime period (one full to-and-fro swing)second (s)
Llength of the pendulummetre (m)
gacceleration due to gravity (about 9.8 m/s² on Earth)m/s²

Valid for small angles (below about 10°). Notice that the mass does not appear in the formula.

What affects the period?
  • Length L: a longer pendulum has a longer period (swings more slowly).
  • Gravity g: weaker gravity makes the period longer.
  • The mass of the bob and the amplitude (for small swings) do not change T.
Why is mass missing? (optional)

For a small angle, sinθ ≈ θ (in radians), and the displacement along the arc is x ≈ Lθ. So the restoring force is F = mg sinθ ≈ mgθ = (mg/L) x. This looks like Hooke’s law F = kx with k = mg/L. Put it into the spring formula: T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(m ÷ (mg/L)) = 2π√(L/g). The mass m cancels out.

Pendulum calculations

We use g = 9.8 m/s² and 4π² ≈ 39.478. (If your teacher uses g = 10 m/s², your answers will differ slightly in the last digit.)

Rearranging for L and g

  1. Start: T = 2π√(L/g)
  2. Divide both sides by 2π: T/(2π) = √(L/g)
  3. Square both sides: T²/(4π²) = L/g

Textbook topic: Ball and bowl system

The ball and bowl system

Put a small ball inside a smooth bowl. Release it from point A on one side. It rolls down to the lowest point O, climbs up the other side to B, and returns. It keeps moving to and fro. This is another example of SHM.

A ball moving in a bowl of radius R between A and B. O is the lowest point (mean position).

Why does it oscillate?

  • The ball’s weight w = mg acts straight down.
  • On the slope, part of the weight acts along the surface and pulls the ball towards O. This is the restoring force.
  • The other part pushes the ball against the bowl and is balanced by the bowl’s push on the ball.
  • For small displacements, the restoring force is nearly proportional to the displacement from O. So the motion is SHM.
  • The ball behaves like a pendulum whose length is the radius R of the bowl. For a small ball moving without friction, T is about 2π√(R/g). (Real rolling balls are a little slower, so treat this as approximate.)

Energy changes

PositionHeightSpeedEnergy
A or BHighestZeroAll potential energy
OLowestMaximumAll kinetic energy

As the ball goes from A to O, potential energy changes into kinetic energy. From O to B, kinetic energy changes back into potential energy. In an ideal bowl with no friction the total energy stays the same and the ball would reach the same height on the other side.

In real life

A real ball slowly stops, because of friction and air resistance. That leads us to the next lesson: damped oscillations.

Knowledge check

Textbook topic: Damped oscillations (Figs. 10.21 and 10.22, shock absorber)

Damped oscillations

Push a swing once and leave it. It does not swing for ever. Each swing is a little smaller than the last, until it stops. Why? Because of air resistance and friction at the support. They take energy from the swing and change it into heat.

Definition: Damped oscillation

An oscillation whose amplitude gradually decreases with time because energy is lost (to friction, air resistance or other resistive forces). The oscillation is damped.

See the difference

Choose a kind of oscillation. Watch the displacement–time graph.

Light damping: the swings get smaller and smaller, but the time for each oscillation stays almost the same.

Where is damping useful? Shock absorbers

A motorcycle, car or bus has springs between the wheels and the body. On a bumpy road the springs compress and then bounce again and again. Without damping the vehicle would keep bouncing and the ride would be dangerous and uncomfortable. So engineers add a shock absorber, a cylinder filled with oil. The oil resists the up-and-down motion and quickly takes away the energy, so the bouncing dies out fast.

A vehicle suspension. The spring supports the load and absorbs the bump. The shock absorber damps the bouncing.
Summary
  • Ideal oscillations (no friction) keep the same amplitude for ever.
  • Real oscillations are damped: amplitude falls, energy changes to heat.
  • Shock absorbers use damping on purpose to give a smooth ride.

Knowledge check

Extra help: avoid these mistakes

Common mistakes (and how to avoid them)

Many students lose marks for the same reasons. Read each card. Ask yourself: “Have I ever thought this?”

1. “A wave carries water (or air) along with it.”

Truth: A wave carries energy. The particles only vibrate around their own places.
Tip: Remember the cork that only bobs up and down.

2. “Amplitude is the distance from crest to trough.”

Truth: Amplitude is from the rest line to the crest. Crest to trough is twice the amplitude.
Tip: Always start measuring from the middle line.

3. “Wavelength is crest to trough.”

Truth: Wavelength is crest to the next crest (one full wave).
Tip: If you count N crests from the first to the last, there are only (N − 1) wavelengths between them.

4. “Period and frequency are the same.”

Truth: They are reciprocals: T = 1/f. Period is in seconds, frequency is in hertz.
Tip: If the answer for T is bigger than 1 s, f must be smaller than 1 Hz.

5. “Sound is a transverse wave.”

Truth: Sound in air is a longitudinal wave (compressions and rarefactions).
Tip: Think of a loudspeaker pushing the air forward and backward.

6. “Waves can only be mechanical.” / “Light needs air.”

Truth: Light and other EM waves need no medium. Sound does need one.
Tip: Ask: “Can it cross space?”

7. “Angle of incidence is measured from the barrier.”

Truth: It is measured from the normal. If the wave makes 30° with the barrier, i = 60°.
Tip: First always draw the normal (dotted line at 90° to the barrier).

8. “When waves enter shallow water the frequency decreases.”

Truth: Frequency stays the same. Speed and wavelength decrease.
Tip: The source decides frequency. The medium decides speed.

9. “Wave speed increases if I increase the frequency.”

Truth: In the same medium the speed is fixed. A higher frequency gives a shorter wavelength.
Tip: v = fλ; if f goes up and v stays fixed, λ goes down.

10. “Destructive interference destroys energy.”

Truth: Energy is not lost. It moves to places where the interference is constructive.

11. “A heavier pendulum bob swings faster.” / “A bigger swing takes longer.”

Truth: For small swings, T depends only on L and g. Mass and amplitude do not change T.
Tip: Look at T = 2π√(L/g). There is no m and no amplitude in it.

12. “At the end of the swing, speed is maximum.”

Truth: At the end points the speed is zero (it turns back). Speed is maximum at the mean position.

13. Forgetting to change units.

Truth: Convert cm to m before using formulae. Write the unit with every final answer.
Tip: Check: Hz × m = m/s.

4. Distinctions students confuse

  • Transverse vs longitudinal: across vs along.
  • Amplitude vs crest-to-trough distance: half vs whole.
  • Period vs frequency: inverse of each other.
  • Constructive vs destructive: add up vs cancel out.

5. SHM quick facts

  • At the extremes: displacement and restoring force maximum, speed zero.
  • At the mean position: displacement and force zero, speed maximum.
  • Spring: bigger m → bigger T; bigger k → smaller T.
  • Pendulum: T depends on L and g only (small angles).
  • Ball in bowl: SHM like a pendulum of length R; energy changes between potential and kinetic.
